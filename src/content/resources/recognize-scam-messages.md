Scam messages come in endless variations — a parcel that couldn't be
delivered, a bank account that has been "locked", a relative in trouble who
needs money right now. But underneath, most of them follow the same pattern:
**they create pressure so you act before you think, and they ask you to act in
a way you normally wouldn't.**

Once you can see the pattern, the details matter much less. This guide is
written to be shared. Read it together, print it, or send it to someone you
look out for.

## Warning signs

### It is urgent, frightening, or exciting

"Your account will be closed today." "You've won." "Pay now to avoid a fine."
Strong emotion is the tool: it pushes you to act quickly. Genuine
organizations give you time and other ways to deal with a problem.

### It arrived out of the blue

You weren't expecting a delivery, didn't enter a competition, and haven't
spoken to the bank recently — yet here is a message asking you to do
something. Unexpected requests deserve extra suspicion, even when the logo and
wording look right.

### It asks for an unusual way to pay

Requests to pay with gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or by sending
money to a "safe account" are a major red flag. Real businesses and government
agencies don't ask to be paid with gift cards.

### It asks for a code

Never share a one-time passcode or verification code with anyone who contacts
you. Those codes exist to prove that *you* are the one logging in or approving
a payment — someone asking for one is almost always trying to get into your
account.

### The link or sender doesn't quite match

Look closely at email addresses and links: an extra word, a misspelling, or an
unfamiliar domain ending can give a fake away. Shortened links hide where they
really go. When in doubt, don't tap the link at all.

### It asks you to keep it secret

"Don't tell your parents." "Keep this between us." Scammers want to stop you
from talking to someone who would recognize what is happening. A request for
secrecy is a reason to tell someone.

## What to do when something doesn't feel right

1. **Pause.** Nothing legitimate is so urgent that you can't take five
   minutes.
2. **Don't use the contact details in the message.** Don't tap the link, reply,
   or call the number it gives.
3. **Check independently.** Contact the organization using a phone number,
   app, or website you already know — the number on the back of your bank
   card, for example.
4. **Talk to someone.** Show the message to a family member or friend. A second
   pair of eyes is one of the best defenses there is.

## If you already responded

Act quickly, and don't be embarrassed — these schemes are designed by people
who do this full-time.

- **If you paid or shared bank details:** call your bank or card provider
  straight away, using the number on your card.
- **If you shared a password or code:** change the password for that account
  (and anywhere else you reused it), and turn on two-step verification.
- **If you installed something at a caller's request:** disconnect the device
  from the internet and get help from someone you trust before using it for
  banking again.

Then report it. In the United States you can report fraud to the Federal Trade
Commission at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). In the
United Kingdom you can forward suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk. In
both countries you can forward scam texts to **7726** with many mobile
carriers. Elsewhere, look for your national consumer protection or cybercrime
agency.

## A simple family agreement

A few household rules go a long way:

- **Agree on a family code word** to confirm it really is you when someone
  calls or messages in an "emergency".
- **Make it normal to check.** Nobody gets in trouble for asking "is this
  real?" — ever.
- **Turn on two-step verification** for email, banking, and the accounts that
  matter most.
- **Keep devices updated**, so known security holes get fixed.

Scams change constantly, but the pattern rarely does. Pressure, surprise,
secrecy, and an unusual way to pay: when you spot them, stop and check.
